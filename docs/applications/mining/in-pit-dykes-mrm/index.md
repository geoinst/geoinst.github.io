# Case Study — Instrumenting In-Pit Tailings Dykes at Muskeg River Mine (2013)

!!! note "About this case study"

    This page is a **digest and full Vietnamese translation** of the 2013 Canadian
    Geotechnical Society conference paper *Application of Geotechnical
    Instrumentation in Monitoring In-Pit Dykes Performance*, by **K.W. Soe Moe** and
    **K.W. Biggar** (BGC Engineering Inc., Edmonton) with **S. Martens** and
    **R. Hoda** (Shell Canada Ltd., Calgary). The paper and its figures remain the
    copyright of the authors and of the Canadian Geotechnical Society; it is
    reproduced here for education and reference alongside an independent
    Vietnamese translation. Cite the original English paper in any formal work.

**In-pit dykes** are the least-discussed and one of the most demanding
instrumentation problems in mine waste management. An in-pit dyke (IPD) is a
tailings-retaining embankment built *inside* a mined-out pit, separating one
tailings cell from the next. Unlike an external tailings dam, it is founded on a
floor that has been **unloaded by up to 90 m of excavation**, it is built from
whatever waste the mine is stripping at the time, and it has to be raised
**continuously while tailings are being deposited behind it** — so the
instruments buried in it must survive being extended upward through the fill,
year after year, by the very equipment that is building the dyke.

This paper, from Shell Canada Energy's **Muskeg River Mine (MRM)** about 70 km
north of Fort McMurray, Alberta, is one of the few published accounts of how that
is actually done at scale: dykes 60–90 m high, built on a foundation that has
been unloaded and reloaded, monitored well enough that the design itself could be
**relaxed as the data came in**.

---

## What the paper establishes

The dykes are designed by the **Observational Approach** (Peck, 1969), classified
"Very High" consequence under the Canadian Dam Association (2007) guidelines.
That classification is what makes the instrumentation load-bearing: the design
assumes *most-likely* material parameters, and the monitoring programme is the
mechanism that proves the assumption — or buys the time to act on a
pre-planned remedy.

## The three results worth carrying away

### 1. The fill behaved better than designed — and the design was relaxed

Initial design pore pressure ratios were *r*<sub>u</sub> = 0.5 (core), 0.3 (shell)
and 0.4 (toe berm). Measured values overwhelmingly sat **below 0.3**. Because
oil-sands fill is placed at its *natural* fluid content and compacted by
fully-loaded haul trucks, it is denser and drier of optimum than conventional
embankment fill — so it generates less construction pore pressure than civil-side
experience predicted. Monitoring the construction let the team **select
*r*<sub>u</sub> values appropriate to the actual stress range**, which the paper
describes as "an overall improvement in the fill utilization of the designs."

### 2. The foundation barely responded — because it had been unloaded

Measured *B* values were 0.05–0.15 in the McMurray members and 0.1–0.3 in the DW
Paleosol. The paper attributes this to **stress relief from excavating up to 90 m
of material before the dyke was built**, and warns that a higher *B* response
should be expected once dyke elevation passes original ground level. The
high-permeability LM1w basal water sands showed **no response at all** to dyke
construction — but did respond to fluid levels in the tailings cells.

### 3. Caution limits were tied to a rate, not a displacement

**Table 5** is the transferable artefact. It sets four independent,
rate-and-strain-based bands with an explicit monitoring and construction
response attached to each:

| Rate of movement (mm/day) | Total shear strain (%) | Level | Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| < 0.07 | < 0.5 | **Green** | Normal construction; bi-weekly monitoring |
| > 0.07 – < 0.2 | > 0.5 – < 1.0 | **Yellow** | Increase monitoring to weekly / twice weekly |
| > 0.2 – < 1.0 | > 1.0 – < 2.0 | **Orange** | Increase to twice weekly / daily |
| > 1.0 | > 2.0 | **Red** | **Stop dyke construction** in that area until the rate falls |

Note the structure: the trigger is a **velocity**, and the top band's action is a
**construction stoppage**, not an alarm. That is the same logic the site's
[ADAQS trigger-action framework](../../../vi/adaqs/index.md) is built on, and it is
why the paper reports that no discrete shear zones ever developed — construction
was stopped or slowed before one could.

## Why this paper matters in this knowledge base

- It is the **mine-side companion** to [Tailings Dam Safety](../index.md) and
  [Tailings Dam Safety (ICOLD Bulletin 194)](../../../reference-manuals/tailings-dam-safety/index.md),
  which treat external tailings facilities. In-pit containment has a different
  foundation history and a different construction sequence.
- Its **caution-limit table** is a worked, published example of the trigger logic
  described generically in [Getting Started](../../../getting-started/index.md).
- Its **total-stress and *K*<sub>o</sub>** section is one of the few field
  datasets linking instrumented earth pressure cells to a **hydraulic fracture**
  assessment — a failure mode that matters wherever low-stress zones develop
  near steep abutments.
- Its **Observational Approach** framing (Peck, 1969) is the same spine that runs
  through [Dunnicliff](../../../reference-manuals/dunnicliff/index.md) and the
  [FHWA](../../../reference-manuals/fhwa/index.md) reference manual.

## How to use this case study

- Start with **§5** (instrumentation and protection) if you are planning a
  programme on a structure that will be raised during monitoring — the
  protection-mound and "no vertical extensions in the core zone" rules are
  practical and rarely written down.
- Use **Table 5** as a starting template for your own trigger table, then
  re-derive the numbers for your materials, your construction rate and your
  consequence classification. Do not copy the thresholds.
- Read **§6** before specifying a pore pressure design value for compacted
  oil-sands or similarly placed fill; the conventional Proctor-based assumption
  is conservative here.
- Cross-link into the [Mining Geotechnics overview](../index.md) for slope radar,
  GNSS and microseismic methods that complement the buried instruments described
  here.

> **Safety and currency.** The data in this paper describe IPD 1 and IPD 2 as
> designed **at the time of writing in 2013**; the authors note the parameters
> were being updated as new data became available. Remote monitoring, GNSS and
> distributed fibre-optic sensing have all advanced since. Treat the
> **observational logic and the caution-limit structure** as durable, and
> **derive your own thresholds** from current site data, current CDA/MAC guidance
> and applicable regulation before using them for design or a regulatory
> submission.
