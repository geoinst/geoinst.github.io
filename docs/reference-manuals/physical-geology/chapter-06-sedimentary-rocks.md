# Chapter 6 — Sediments and Sedimentary Rocks

Sedimentary rock is where most civil engineering happens. It covers about three-quarters of
the continental surface, it hosts most aquifers, and its **bedding planes** are the
commonest planes of weakness an engineer will meet.

## 6.1 Clastic sedimentary rocks

Formed from fragments of pre-existing rock, classified by **grain size**:

- **Gravel / conglomerate / breccia** — > 2 mm
- **Sand / sandstone** — 0.063–2 mm
- **Silt / siltstone** — 0.004–0.063 mm
- **Clay / mudstone / shale** — < 0.004 mm

Beyond grain size, three properties control behaviour: **sorting**, **rounding** and
**cementation**. A well-sorted, well-cemented sandstone is a good foundation; a poorly
sorted, weakly cemented mudstone is not.

## 6.2 Chemical and organic sedimentary rocks

Precipitated from solution or built by organisms — limestone, dolomite, chert, evaporites
and coal. **Limestone** is the critical one for foundations, because it dissolves.

## 6.3 Depositional environments and bedding

Sediment is deposited in environments (fluvial, lacustrine, marine, deltaic, aeolian,
glacial) that leave characteristic **structures**: bedding, cross-bedding, graded bedding,
ripple marks, mud cracks. These are not decoration — they tell the engineer how the deposit
varies laterally and where the weak layers are.

## Key points for the geotechnical engineer

- **Grain size alone does not classify a soil** — sorting, rounding and cementation matter
  just as much.
- **Bedding planes are planes of weakness**, and their dip relative to a cut or foundation
  governs stability.
- **Limestone dissolves** — expect karst, cavities and erratic groundwater.
- **Depositional environment predicts variability**: a delta is far less uniform than an
  aeolian sand.
