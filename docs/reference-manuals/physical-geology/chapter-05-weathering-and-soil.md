# Chapter 5 — Weathering and Soil

This is the chapter that matters most to a geotechnical engineer: **weathering is the
process that converts rock into soil**. Every residual soil, every weathered profile, and
every "soft" zone in a rock mass is the product of it.

## 5.1 Mechanical weathering

Rock is broken physically, without changing its minerals.

- **Unloading / exfoliation** — removal of overburden lets the rock expand; it cracks
  *parallel to the new ground surface*.
- **Frost wedging** — water in cracks freezes, expands by ~9 %, and prises the rock apart.
  Most effective where freezing and thawing alternate frequently.
- **Salt crystallisation** — salts grow in pores and push grains apart (honeycomb
  weathering).
- **Roots and burrowing animals** — roots widen every crack they enter.

## 5.2 Chemical weathering

Rock is decomposed by reaction with water, oxygen and acids. The key reactions are
**hydrolysis** (feldspar → clay minerals), **oxidation** (iron minerals → rust-coloured
oxides) and **dissolution** (carbonates and evaporites). Chemical weathering is fastest in
warm, wet climates — which is why **deep weathering profiles are the norm in Vietnam**.

## 5.3 Soil formation

Soil is a mixture of mineral grains, organic matter, air and water. The controls on how
soil develops are **climate, parent material, slope and time** — the same four factors that
determine whether a site has a thick residual soil or bare rock.

## 5.4 Soil horizons

A mature soil profile develops distinct horizons — O (organic), A (mixed organic-mineral),
E (leached), B (accumulation) and C (incompletely weathered parent material). For the
engineer the significance is that **each horizon has different strength, permeability and
compressibility**, and the water table commonly sits near a horizon boundary.

## Key points for the geotechnical engineer

- **Weathering defines the ground profile.** The depth of weathering, not the rock type, is
  often what decides foundation depth.
- **Mechanical and chemical weathering act together** — frost opens the cracks that water
  then hydrolyses.
- **Residual soil strength decreases upward** as weathering increases; the transition to
  sound rock is gradational, not sharp.
- **Karst is chemical weathering at landscape scale.**
