# Chapter 3 — Intrusive Igneous Rocks

Igneous rock forms when molten material cools and crystallises. **Where** it cools decides
its texture, and therefore much of its engineering behaviour.

## 3.1 The rock cycle

Rock is not permanent. Igneous rock is weathered and eroded into sediment; sediment is
buried and lithified into sedimentary rock; heat and pressure turn either into metamorphic
rock; and any of them can be melted again. The cycle is why **the soil above a rock and the
rock itself can have completely different origins**.

## 3.2 Intrusive versus extrusive

- **Intrusive** (plutonic) — magma solidifies underground. Slow cooling → **coarse,
  interlocking crystals** (granite, diorite, gabbro). Strong when intact; the joints, not
  the crystals, control the mass.
- **Extrusive** (volcanic) — lava solidifies at the surface. Fast cooling → **fine-grained
  or glassy** (basalt, andesite, rhyolite), often with vesicles and flow structures.

## 3.3 Intrusive bodies

Batholiths, stocks, dykes and sills. A **dyke** cuts across existing layers; a **sill**
lies parallel to them. Both are discontinuities in the ground model, and both can be
either stronger or weaker than the host rock.

## Key points for the geotechnical engineer

- **Texture tells the cooling history** — and coarse igneous rock tends to be strong but
  joint-controlled.
- **Intrusive contacts are discontinuities.** A dyke or sill is a plane where properties
  change abruptly, and often a plane of weakness.
- **Extrusive rock can be highly porous or fractured** — vesicular basalt and fractured
  volcanic rock can be surprisingly permeable.
- **Weathered basalt becomes laterite**, one of the characteristic problem soils of
  Vietnam's Central Highlands and Southeast.
