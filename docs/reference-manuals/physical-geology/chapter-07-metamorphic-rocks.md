# Chapter 7 — Metamorphism and Metamorphic Rocks

Metamorphism changes rock in the solid state, under heat and pressure, without melting it.
The result is a rock with a **new fabric** — and that fabric is the most important single
cause of anisotropy in rock engineering.

## 7.1 Controls on metamorphism

The agents are **heat**, **pressure** (both confining and directed), and **chemically
active fluids**. Increasing grade produces a sequence of minerals, which is why
metamorphic rocks can be mapped by their mineral assemblage.

## 7.2 Foliation

Directed pressure aligns platy minerals (mica, chlorite) into **foliation** — a planar
fabric. Foliation is the defining feature of slate, schist and gneiss.

## 7.3 Types of metamorphism

- **Regional** — large areas, associated with mountain building and plate collision;
  produces foliated rock.
- **Contact** — local, around an igneous intrusion; produces non-foliated rock (hornfels,
  marble, quartzite).
- **Hydrothermal** — alteration by hot fluids, often producing weak, clay-rich zones.

## Key points for the geotechnical engineer

- **Foliation creates extreme anisotropy.** Schist and slate are far weaker across the
  foliation than along it — and the foliation orientation decides slope and foundation
  behaviour.
- **Marble and quartzite are strong and isotropic** when fresh, but can be karstic (marble)
  or intensely jointed.
- **Hydrothermal alteration produces weak zones** — the same process that makes a rock mass
  locally unbuildable.
- Metamorphic terrain usually means **steep slopes, deep weathering and structural
  complexity**.
