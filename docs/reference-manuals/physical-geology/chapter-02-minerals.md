# Chapter 2 — Minerals

Minerals are the building blocks of rock, and a small number of them dominate the Earth's
crust. Recognising them is the first practical skill of field geology — and the reason a
borehole log can say "weathered granite" rather than "grey stuff".

## 2.1 What defines a mineral

A mineral is **naturally occurring**, has a **definite chemical composition** (or a limited
range), and has an **ordered atomic structure** (a lattice). Atoms are held in the lattice
by ionic and covalent bonding.

## 2.2 The silicate minerals

Silicates make up the great majority of crustal rock. They are built from the
**silica tetrahedron** (SiO₄), combined in different ways — isolated, chains, sheets,
frameworks — and that internal arrangement controls how the mineral behaves, including how
it cleaves and how it weathers.

- **Framework silicates** — quartz, feldspar. Hard, resistant; quartz survives weathering
  and becomes the sand fraction of soil.
- **Sheet silicates** — mica, clay minerals, chlorite. Cleave into sheets and are the
  weak, water-sensitive minerals in soil and rock.
- **Chain silicates** — amphibole, pyroxene.

## 2.3 Non-silicate minerals

Carbonates (calcite, dolomite), oxides (hematite, magnetite), sulphides, sulphates
(gypsum) and halides. Calcite and dolomite matter enormously for foundations because they
**dissolve**, producing karst.

## Key points for the geotechnical engineer

- **Quartz is the survivor.** It resists weathering, which is why sand is quartz-rich and
  why quartz content predicts a granular soil's behaviour.
- **Clay minerals come from silicates that did not survive.** They are the weathering
  product of feldspar and mica — and the source of most soil problems.
- **Carbonates dissolve.** Any rock containing calcite or dolomite can form karst.
- Mineral identification is what makes a **field description** meaningful and defensible.
