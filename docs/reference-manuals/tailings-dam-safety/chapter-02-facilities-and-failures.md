# Chapter 2 — Tailings Facilities and How They Fail

## 2.1 Tailings facility types

Tailings are deposited as a slurry and the supernatant water is recovered through
a **decant** system. The embankment that retains the deposit is raised by one of
several methods, which strongly influence safety:

- **Downstream method** — each raise is placed downstream of the previous one,
  producing a broad, stable cross-section with foundation support under most of
  the structure. Generally the most robust.
- **Centerline method** — raises are placed on the centerline; intermediate in
  risk.
- **Upstream method** — each raise is placed on the upstream (deposition) side,
  relying on the tailings beach for support. **Highest risk**: the embankment
  sits on the weakest, often loose, saturated material, and is prone to static
  and seismic liquefaction.

Alternative configurations include **paddock / in-pit** storage and **thickened
/ paste / filtered** tailings, which reduce free water and lower liquefaction
risk.

## 2.2 The dominant failure modes

Understanding how tailings facilities fail focuses the monitoring program. The
principal modes are:

1. **Static liquefaction of the embankment or deposit.** Loose, saturated,
   contractive tailings lose strength under static loading (e.g., a raise, a
   rapid rise in phreatic surface, or a foundation weakness) and flow. This is
   the mechanism behind several of the worst historical failures.
2. **Seismic liquefaction.** Cyclic shaking converts loose saturated tailings
   into a liquefied state, triggering flow sliding. Seismic regions demand
   special attention to liquefiable layers.
3. **Overtopping.** Inadequate freeboard, extraordinary rainfall, pond
   mismanagement, or blocked decants let water overtop the embankment.
4. **Piping / internal erosion.** Seepage erodes a channel through or under the
   embankment, often undetected until a blowout.
5. **Slope instability (static).** Over-steep beaches or embankments fail by
   sliding, especially where pore pressure is high.
6. **Foundation failure.** Weak or variable foundations (e.g., compressible
   soils,karst, or liquefiable layers) lead to settlement, cracking, or flow.
7. **Structural failure of appurtenant works.** Decant towers, pipelines, and
   spillways can fail and compromise the whole system.

## 2.3 The chain to failure

Most catastrophic failures are not single events but chains: a predisposing
condition (loose saturated tailings, high phreatic surface) plus a trigger
(rainfall, rapid filling, earthquake) plus inadequate detection. Monitoring
exists to break that chain — by revealing the predisposing condition early.

!!! warning "Upstream construction deserves special vigilance"
    Facilities using the upstream method carry the highest inherent risk and are
    the subject of global efforts to prohibit or strictly control them. Their
    surveillance programs should be the most intensive, with particular attention
    to pore pressure and beach geometry.

## 2.4 Linking failure modes to instruments

| Failure mode | Primary signals | Instruments |
|-------------|----------------|------------|
| Static / seismic liquefaction | Rising pore pressure, low SPT/CPT resistance, settlement | Piezometers, inclinometers, survey, CPT |
| Overtopping | Loss of freeboard, high pond level | Survey, level gauges, rainfall |
| Piping | Uplift, muddy seepage, settlement | Piezometers, seepage weirs, visual |
| Slope instability | Movement, cracking | Survey, inclinometers, extensometers |
| Foundation failure | Differential settlement, tilt | Survey, piezometers, tiltmeters |

## 2.5 Key takeaways

- Construction method governs inherent risk; upstream is highest.
- Liquefaction (static and seismic) is the dominant catastrophic mechanism.
- Failures are chains of predisposition + trigger + late detection.
- Map every failure mode to specific instruments before designing the program.
