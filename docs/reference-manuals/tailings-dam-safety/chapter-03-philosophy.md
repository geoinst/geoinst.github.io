# Chapter 3 — Monitoring Philosophy for Tailings

## 3.1 Two complementary pillars

Tailings surveillance rests on two pillars that are **complementary, not
substitutes**:

1. **Visual surveillance** — trained personnel observing the facility: beach
   geometry, seepage clarity and location, cracks, slumps, animal burrows,
   decant condition, vegetation changes, and signs of distress.
2. **Instrumentation** — survey monuments, piezometers, inclinometers,
   extensometers, seepage weirs, and remote-sensing systems that quantify
   behavior over time and space.

Neither alone is sufficient. Visual surveillance detects things instruments
cannot (a new seep, a sinkhole, a cracked decant). Instrumentation detects
things the eye cannot (a slow pore-pressure rise, sub-millimeter creep,
regional deformation invisible from the surface).

## 3.2 Why visual surveillance leads

For tailings facilities, routine, competent **visual inspection is the single
most valuable surveillance activity**. An experienced inspector walking the
beach and crest will notice a softening toe, a turbid seep, or an unusual
settlement long before instruments raise an alarm. Instruments are a force
multiplier, not a replacement.

A strong program therefore schedules:

- **Frequent routine patrols** (daily to weekly depending on risk and stage).
- **Detailed periodic inspections** by a qualified tailings engineer.
- **Event-driven inspections** after storms, earthquakes, or rapid filling.

## 3.3 Why instrumentation is indispensable

Some of the most dangerous signals are invisible:

- Pore pressure building in a loose deposit.
- Internal deformation along a shear surface.
- Regional ground movement detectable only by satellite radar.

Instrumentation provides **continuous, quantitative, defensible** records and
enables automated alarms that operate when no one is on site. For high-consequence
facilities, a mix of manual and automated instruments is essential.

## 3.4 The comparison at the heart of monitoring

All monitoring is a comparison:

> **Measured behavior** − **Expected behavior** = **Performance signal**

Expected behavior comes from design analyses and the baseline established in the
early life of the facility. A small, explained difference is normal; a large,
unexplained, or trending difference is an anomaly demanding evaluation.

## 3.5 Designing the balance

The balance between visual and instrumental surveillance is set by:

- **Consequence category** (higher consequence → more instrumentation, more
  automation).
- **Construction method** (upstream → intensive).
- **Seismicity and climate** (extreme events → event-driven instruments).
- **Stage of life** (commissioning, peak filling, closure each need emphasis).

!!! tip "Document the philosophy"
    The surveillance plan should state explicitly what is monitored visually,
    what by instrument, the rationale, and the response to each anomaly type.
    This is developed in [Chapter 4](chapter-04-planning.md).

## 3.6 Key takeaways

- Visual surveillance and instrumentation are complementary pillars.
- Competent visual inspection is the single most valuable activity.
- Instruments detect invisible, continuous, and regional signals.
- The program's balance is set by consequence, method, and hazard.
