# Chapter 14 — Case Histories

!!! note "Purpose of this chapter"
    These summaries describe **publicly reported lessons** from significant
    tailings incidents at a general, factual level — what was learned about
    monitoring and surveillance, not proprietary detail. They are included to
    build engineering judgment. Always consult the official investigation reports
    and the applicable regulatory guidance for authoritative accounts.

## 14.1 Lessons shared across incidents

Reviewing major tailings failures reveals recurring themes directly relevant to
this reference:

- **Liquefaction of loose, saturated tailings** is the dominant catastrophic
  mechanism, especially for upstream-construction facilities.
- **Early signals were often present** — rising pore pressure, movement, or
  changing seepage — but were not always detected, communicated, or acted upon.
- **Visual surveillance and instrument data together** would have revealed more
  than either alone.
- **Consequence of failure is extreme**, justifying intensive, independent
  monitoring for high-consequence facilities.

## 14.2 Illustrative examples (generalized)

Several widely cited incidents illustrate the themes of this book:

- **A large upstream-facility flow failure** demonstrated how static
  liquefaction of the embankment and deposit can produce long run-out and
  widespread downstream impact when pore pressure and beach management are not
  controlled.
- **A tailings dam failure triggered by concentrated seepage and internal
  erosion** showed how undetected piping can breach an embankment, and how
  freeboard and seepage monitoring are critical.
- **A failure following extreme rainfall** highlighted the role of freeboard,
  decant management, and event-driven monitoring during storms.
- **A seismically triggered liquefaction** underscored the need to characterize
  liquefiable layers and monitor post-earthquake pore pressure.

(These are described in general terms; the official investigation reports for each
event contain the authoritative technical record.)

## 14.3 What good look like

Facilities that perform well over decades share habits: competent routine
inspection, instruments tied to documented failure modes, validated and archived
data, predefined action levels, independent review, and a culture where raising a
concern is rewarded, not penalized.

## 14.4 Applying the lessons

For your facility, revisit [Chapter 4](chapter-04-planning.md): are the instruments
tied to the real failure modes, are action levels predefined, and would an
accelerating signal be detected and acted upon in time? The case histories answer
"why this matters" — the rest of this reference answers "how."

## 14.5 Key takeaways

- Liquefaction of loose saturated tailings dominates catastrophic failures.
- Early signals are common but must be detected, communicated, and acted upon.
- Visual and instrumental surveillance are both necessary.
- Predefined action levels and a speaking-up culture save lives.
