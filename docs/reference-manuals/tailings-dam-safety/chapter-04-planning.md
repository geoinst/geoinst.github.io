# Chapter 4 — Planning a Tailings Monitoring Program

## 4.1 Planning is a process, not a checklist

A monitoring program that is pasted onto a facility after construction is weak.
The strongest programs are planned **risk-informed and consequence-driven** from
the earliest stages of design and revised as the facility evolves. Planning
answers four questions before any instrument is bought:

1. **What could go wrong?** (failure modes — [Chapter 2](chapter-02-facilities-and-failures.md))
2. **What would the early warning look like?** (the measurable signal of each mode)
3. **What decisions will the data support?** (trigger levels, responses)
4. **What is the right balance of visual and instrumental surveillance?** ([Chapter 3](chapter-03-philosophy.md))

## 4.2 Anchor the plan to failure modes

Every instrument should trace back to a specific failure mode or a design
assumption being confirmed. A pore-pressure monitor on a downstream toe exists
because static liquefaction and piping are credible; a crest survey exists
because slope instability and settlement are credible. If an instrument cannot
be tied to a mode or a decision, question its value.

!!! tip "Modes first, instruments second"
    Resist the temptation to deploy "what we always use." Start from the
    facility's specific hazards — upstream construction, seismicity, weak
    foundation, high consequence — and let those dictate the program.

## 4.3 Consequence and risk categorization

Most modern tailings standards require categorizing facilities by the
**potential consequence of failure** (often A/B/C or similar) and the **likelihood**
given the design and controls. The categorization drives:

- Frequency of inspection
- Degree of automation and real-time alerting
- Number and distribution of instruments
- Independence and review rigor

Higher-consequence facilities warrant **independent, real-time** monitoring with
redundant sensing, while lower-consequence facilities may rely more on competent
manual surveillance.

## 4.4 Elements of the surveillance plan

A complete plan documents:

- **Parameters monitored** (piezometric head, deformation, seepage, pond level,
  beach geometry, rainfall).
- **Instrument type, location, and depth** with rationale.
- **Baseline and frequency** of readings.
- **Trigger, action, and alarm levels** ([Chapter 13](chapter-13-decisions.md)).
- **Roles and responsibilities** for reading, reviewing, and responding.
- **Data validation and archiving** procedures ([Chapter 11](chapter-11-data-management.md)).
- **Review and update** cycle, especially after raises, storms, or earthquakes.

## 4.5 Staged implementation

Because a TSF is built over decades, the monitoring program is staged:

- **Pre-impoundment / commissioning** — baseline survey, foundation instruments,
  establish datum.
- **Operating life** — expand with each raise; densify where signals appear.
- **Closure** — maintain surveillance through and after closure until the
  post-closure steady state is demonstrated.

## 4.6 Key takeaways

- Plan risk-informed and consequence-driven, not by habit.
- Every instrument traces to a failure mode or a decision.
- Consequence category sets the intensity and automation of monitoring.
- The program is staged across the facility's full life, including closure.
