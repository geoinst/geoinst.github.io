# Chapter 3 — Systematic Approach to Planning Monitoring Programs

## 3.1 The hub of the discipline

If this book had a single most important chapter, it is this one. A monitoring
program planned systematically — rather than by habit or by copying a neighbor's
layout — is far more likely to produce useful, defensible data.

## 3.2 Define the geotechnical questions first

Before selecting any instrument, state the questions the program must answer,
such as:

- Will the excavation wall exceed an allowable movement?
- Is pore pressure building where it could trigger instability?
- Is the embankment settling as predicted?
- Is the tunnel crown converging within acceptable limits?

Each question implies a parameter, a location, and a decision.

## 3.3 The systematic steps (abridged)

A disciplined plan works through, in outline:

1. Define the project and the geotechnical concerns.
2. List the questions each part of the work must answer.
3. Determine the parameters that answer those questions.
4. Select candidate instruments and compare them.
5. Decide locations, depths, and numbers.
6. Set baseline, frequency, and duration.
7. Specify procurement, installation, and calibration.
8. Define data handling, validation, and interpretation.
9. Assign responsibilities and budget.
10. Plan for the instrument's full life, including removal or handover.

## 3.4 Selecting instruments

Selection weighs:

- **Suitability** for the parameter and environment.
- **Accuracy and range** versus what the decision requires (not maximal, but
  adequate).
- **Durability and maintainability** in the field.
- **Power, telemetry, and cost.**

Over-specifying wastes money; under-specifying produces uninterpretable data.

## 3.5 Budgeting and phasing

Monitoring is cheapest when planned with the project, not bolted on later. Budget
for procurement, installation, reading, validation, and — often forgotten —
long-term maintenance and data management. Phase the program with construction
and operation.

!!! warning "The most common planning failure"
    Installing instruments with no defined question and no plan to read,
    validate, and act on the data. Such instruments are decorations. Planning
    answers: *what question, what decision, who reads, what then?*

## 3.6 Key takeaways

- Plan systematically; define questions before instruments.
- Each question implies a parameter, location, and decision.
- Balance suitability, accuracy, durability, and cost.
- Budget for the instrument's whole life, not just purchase.
