# Chapter 4 — Compaction

## 4.1 What compaction is

**Compaction** is the densification of soil by removing air voids through mechanical
effort — rolling, tamping, or vibration. It **increases dry unit weight**, and with
it **strength and stiffness**, while **reducing permeability and compressibility**.
It does not remove water, so compaction and consolidation are different processes.

## 4.2 The moisture–density relationship

For a given compactive effort, there is an **optimum water content** $w_{opt}$ at
which the soil reaches its **maximum dry unit weight** $\gamma_{d,max}$:

- **Dry of optimum** — the soil is stiff and hard to compact; the particles resist
  rearrangement.
- **Wet of optimum** — water lubricates the particles but fills the voids; density
  falls again.

The **compaction curve** (dry unit weight vs. water content) is the basis of field
control.

## 4.3 Standard and modified Proctor

Two standard laboratory efforts are used:

- **Standard Proctor** — lower energy; used for most fills and embankments.
- **Modified Proctor** — higher energy (heavier hammer, greater drop); used for
  heavily loaded fills, airfields, and pavements.

Higher energy gives a **higher maximum dry unit weight** and a **lower optimum
water content**.

## 4.4 Field control

Field compaction is controlled by specifying a **percentage of the maximum dry unit
weight** (e.g. 95% of Standard Proctor) and a **moisture range** around optimum.
Verification uses:

- **Field density tests** — sand-cone, balloon, or nuclear gauge; and
- **Moisture content** — oven, speedy, or nuclear.

## 4.5 Compaction, structure, and behaviour

Compaction is not just about density:

- **Compacted dry of optimum** — flocculated structure, higher permeability and
  stiffness, lower strength at high strain.
- **Compacted wet of optimum** — dispersed structure, lower permeability, higher
  strength, but more shrinkage potential.
- The **method** (kneading vs. static) also changes the structure.

This matters for clays used as **liners** or **barriers**, where permeability, not
density, governs the design.

## 4.6 Key takeaways

- Compaction removes **air**, increasing dry unit weight, strength, and stiffness,
  and reducing permeability and compressibility.
- There is an **optimum water content** for maximum dry density.
- **Standard** vs. **modified** Proctor differ in energy.
- Field control specifies **percent compaction and moisture range**.
- **Structure** (dry vs. wet of optimum) affects permeability and strength.
