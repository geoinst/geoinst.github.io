# Chapter 3 — Monitoring Philosophy: Visual Surveillance and Instrumentation

## 3.1 Two pillars

Dam monitoring rests on two complementary pillars:

1. **Visual surveillance** — trained observation of the dam, reservoir, and
   appurtenances, on foot and by watercraft or aerial means.
2. **Instrumentation** — engineered sensors that measure specific quantities at
   specific locations, continuously or periodically.

Neither is sufficient alone. The source manual's central message is that dam
performance monitoring is a **balance** of the two.

## 3.2 Visual surveillance

Visual inspection remains the single most valuable monitoring tool. It can detect
things no instrument is pointed at:

- New or growing **cracks** in embankments or concrete.
- **Seepage** appearance — location, amount, turbidity, temperature.
- **Sinkholes, depressions, or bulges** on the dam or abutments.
- **Vegetation changes**, animal burrows, erosion, or slumping.
- Condition of **outlet works, gates, and spillways**.
- Reservoir **floatables, trees, or debris** near intake structures.

!!! tip "Inspection is a measurement"
    A well-run program treats inspection findings as data: dated, located,
    photographed, and trended — not just notes in a logbook.

Surveillance ranges from routine patrols to intensive monitoring after floods,
earthquakes, or the discovery of an anomaly.

## 3.3 Instrumentation

Instruments extend human senses to places and frequencies people cannot reach:

- **Inside the embankment or foundation** (piezometers, extensometers, inclinometers).
- **Continuous or remote** measurement (automated stations in harsh or inaccessible locations).
- **Precise, quantitative** values (millimeter deformation, kPa pore pressure).
- **Event capture** (recording during a quake or storm that no inspector could attend).

## 3.4 How the two complement each other

| Need | Visual surveillance | Instrumentation |
|------|--------------------|----------------|
| Detect a brand-new leak | Strong | Weak (only where instruments exist) |
| Quantify slow settlement | Weak | Strong |
| Confirm a reading is real | Corroborates | Provides the number |
| Cover the whole dam | Strong (if walked) | Point measurements only |
| Monitor during a storm | Limited (safety) | Strong (remote/automated) |

The strongest programs use instruments to **flag** and inspection to **confirm**.
An instrument anomaly is always followed by a field check.

## 3.5 Selecting the balance

The balance between the two depends on:

- **Consequence of failure** — higher consequences justify more instrumentation.
- **Accessibility** — remote or hazardous locations favor automated instruments.
- **Age and condition** — older or distressed dams warrant denser monitoring.
- **Regulatory requirements** — some jurisdictions mandate minimum programs.

## 3.6 When instrumentation cannot help

Instruments cannot detect a failure mode unless one is placed where the mode would
manifest. A program that measures only what is easy to measure, rather than what
the failure modes demand, creates a false sense of security. This is why planning
([Chapter 4](chapter-04-planning.md)) begins with failure modes, not instruments.

## 3.7 Key takeaways

- Monitoring = visual surveillance + instrumentation, in balance.
- Inspection detects what instruments cannot; instruments quantify what inspection cannot.
- Always corroborate an instrument anomaly with a field check.
- Place instruments where failure modes would manifest — never just where it is convenient.
