# Chapter 12 — Data Evaluation, Presentation, and Interpretation

## 12.1 Turning numbers into understanding

Data becomes useful only when evaluated: plotted, compared with the baseline and
design, and interpreted. This chapter is about separating **signal from noise**.

## 12.2 Presentation

Effective plots reveal behavior at a glance:

- **Time-series** — readings vs. date, with reservoir level overlaid.
- **Scatter plots** — seepage vs. reservoir, piezometric head vs. reservoir.
- **Profiles** — piezometric head vs. depth, deformation vs. distance along the crest.
- **Cumulative** — settlement vs. time to show rate.

## 12.3 The baseline and the band

The baseline is the historical normal range. Plotting a **band** around it makes
departures obvious. A reading outside the band is not automatically a problem — but
it is a prompt to investigate.

## 12.4 Separating signal from noise

Real trends must be distinguished from:

- **Temperature effects** — many sensors drift with temperature; correct or isolate them.
- **Seasonal effects** — reservoir and pore pressure cycle annually.
- **Tidal / barometric effects** — coastal or shallow sensors.
- **Instrument noise and faults** — frozen, dropping, or drifting sensors.

## 12.5 Correlating variables

The strongest interpretations correlate measurements: a piezometric rise *with* a
reservoir rise is normal; a piezometric rise *without* one is not. Deformation that
tracks pore-pressure change points to a specific mechanism.

## 12.6 From anomaly to hypothesis

When a departure appears, the engineer forms and tests hypotheses:

1. Is it a sensor fault? (Check validation, neighbors, manual reading.)
2. Is it an environmental effect? (Season, temperature, event.)
3. Is it real dam behavior? (Correlate with other instruments and inspection.)

## 12.7 Documentation of evaluation

Every evaluation — routine or triggered — should be recorded: the plots used, the
conclusion, and any action. This builds an institutional memory of how the dam
"speaks."

## 12.8 Key takeaways

- Plot against time, reservoir, depth, and distance to reveal behavior.
- Use a baseline band; departures prompt investigation, not panic.
- Remove temperature, seasonal, and sensor-noise effects before judging a trend.
- Correlate variables and document every evaluation.
