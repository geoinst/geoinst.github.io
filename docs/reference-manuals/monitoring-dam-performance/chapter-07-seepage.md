# Chapter 7 — Seepage, Leakage, and Phreatic-Surface Monitoring

## 7.1 Seepage is both normal and dangerous

Some seepage through and under a dam is expected and acceptable. The danger is
**change** — increasing flow, turbidity, or seepage through new paths. Seepage
monitoring answers two questions: *how much* and *how clean*.

## 7.2 Measuring quantity

| Method | How | Notes |
|--------|-----|-------|
| **Weir / flume** | Measure depth over a notch, compute flow | Simple, reliable total-flow measurement |
| **Tipping bucket** | Count tips of a known volume | Good for automated, low-flow logging |
| **Container + stopwatch** | Time to fill a known volume | Crude but useful for spot checks |
| **Observation + mapping** | Location, area, wetness | Essential; many leaks are diffuse |

## 7.3 Measuring quality — turbidity

The **clarity** of seepage is often more important than its volume. Clear seepage
through a known path is usually benign; **turbid or sandy** seepage can signal
internal erosion. Simple observations (visual, "jar test") and, increasingly,
turbidity sensors or filter-catch devices provide early warning.

!!! danger "Muddy seepage is a red flag"
    Sudden turbidity, especially after a reservoir rise or quake, can indicate
    internal erosion. It warrants immediate field verification and, often,
    intensified monitoring.

## 7.4 The phreatic surface

The **phreatic surface** (seepage line) inside an embankment is the boundary
between saturated and unsaturated zones. It is inferred from piezometer readings
([Chapter 6](chapter-06-piezometers.md)). A phreatic line that rises above the
design assumption reduces downstream slope stability and may indicate clogged
drains.

## 7.5 Leakage at appurtenances

Conduits, outlet works, and abutment contacts are common seepage paths. Monitor
for wetness, staining, and flow near:

- Conduit inlet and outlet structures.
- Grout curtains and cutoff walls (look for increased downstream seepage).
- Abutment contacts between embankment and foundation or walls.

## 7.7 Establishing a baseline and trend

Track seepage against reservoir level. Plot **seepage vs. reservoir elevation**
for each measurement point. A point that departs upward from its historical band
— especially with turbidity — is an anomaly demanding evaluation
([Chapter 12](chapter-12-evaluation.md)).

## 7.8 Key takeaways

- Monitor seepage *quantity* and *quality*; turbidity often matters more than volume.
- Weirs and tipping buckets quantify flow; observation maps diffuse leaks.
- Track the phreatic surface from piezometers; a rising line reduces slope stability.
- Watch appurtenances; muddy or increasing seepage is a red flag.
