# Chapter 4 — Planning a Dam Monitoring Program

## 4.1 Planning is the hub of the program

A monitoring program is only as good as the thought behind it. The most expensive
mistake is installing instruments that do not answer the questions that matter.
Planning should be a deliberate, documented process.

## 4.2 The systematic approach

A practical planning process follows these steps:

1. **Assemble the team** — owner, dam engineer, geologist, instrument specialist.
2. **Characterize the dam** — type, geometry, foundation, history, consequences.
3. **Identify potential failure modes (PFMs)** — the credible ways the dam could fail ([Chapter 2](chapter-02-failure-modes.md)).
4. **Define the performance questions** — for each PFM, *what would we need to observe to detect movement toward failure?*
5. **Select indicators and locations** — the measurements and their best positions.
6. **Choose instruments** — matching the indicator, environment, and required accuracy.
7. **Define frequencies and thresholds** — how often to read, and what triggers review ([Chapter 9](chapter-09-automated.md), [Chapter 12](chapter-12-evaluation.md)).
8. **Plan installation and validation** — who installs, and how readings are verified.
9. **Plan operation and maintenance** — keeping instruments working for decades.
10. **Plan data management and evaluation** — storage, validation, reporting.
11. **Document the program** — a monitoring plan the next engineer can inherit.

## 4.3 Define the performance questions first

Good plans invert the usual instinct. Instead of *"let's install piezometers,"* the
question is *"how would we detect internal erosion in this foundation?"* — which
then dictates piezometers at specific locations and depths.

| Performance question | Indicator | Instrument |
|---------------------|----------|-----------|
| Is seepage increasing or becoming erosive? | Seepage rate & turbidity | Weirs, observations, piezometers |
| Is the downstream slope stabilizing or moving? | Surface & internal movement | Survey, inclinometer, extensometer |
| Is uplift threatening a gravity dam? | Pore pressure at base | Uplift piezometers, joint meters |

## 4.4 Matching instrument to need

For each indicator, select an instrument family whose accuracy, range, and
durability fit the task. See [Chapter 5](chapter-05-instruments.md) for the menu.
Avoid over-specifying (unnecessary precision raises cost and failure rate) and
under-specifying (insufficient resolution hides the signal).

## 4.5 Frequency and triggers

Reading frequency should reflect the rate of change expected:

- **Seasonal** variables (reservoir, pore pressure) → monthly to quarterly in steady state.
- **Construction or filling** → daily to weekly.
- **After an event** (flood, quake, anomaly) → intensively, even continuously.

Thresholds convert readings into action. A *threshold* is a value or rate of
change that, when crossed, requires review ([Chapter 13](chapter-13-decisions.md)).

## 4.6 Life-cycle cost, not just first cost

A monitoring program's cost is dominated by decades of operation, maintenance, and
data handling — not the initial hardware. Plan for spare parts, calibration,
staff turnover, and data-system obsolescence from the start.

!!! example "Documented plan pays off"
    A written monitoring plan lets the program survive staff changes, supports
    regulator reviews, and makes it obvious *why* each instrument exists — which
    is exactly when budget cuts threaten the wrong ones.

## 4.7 Key takeaways

- Plan from failure modes and performance questions, not from a shopping list.
- Select instruments to match the indicator, location, and required accuracy.
- Set reading frequencies and thresholds deliberately.
- Budget for the full life cycle, and write the plan down.
