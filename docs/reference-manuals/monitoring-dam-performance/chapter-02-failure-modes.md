# Chapter 2 — Dam Performance and Potential Failure Modes

## 2.1 Monitoring is organized around failure modes

The most effective monitoring programs are built **top-down from potential failure
modes (PFMs)** rather than bottom-up from a list of instruments. A *failure mode*
is a credible way the dam could fail, or a condition that could lead to failure.
Once the credible PFMs are identified, the engineer asks: *what measurements would
reveal movement toward that failure mode before it becomes dangerous?*

This is the central planning idea, developed in [Chapter 4](chapter-04-planning.md).

## 2.2 Why dams fail

Most dam failures and incidents trace to a small set of physical mechanisms:

| Mechanism | Typical signature | Primary measurements |
|-----------|------------------|---------------------|
| **Internal erosion / piping** | Seepage carrying soil, muddy water, sinkholes, wet spots | Seepage quantity & clarity, piezometric levels, settlement |
| **Overtopping** | High reservoir, inadequate freeboard | Reservoir level, rainfall, inflow |
| **Sliding / slope instability** | Cracking, bulging, movement of slopes | Deformation survey, inclinometers, piezometers |
| **Foundation failure** | Movement or excessive seepage through foundation | Piezometers, deformation, seepage |
| **Concrete/structural distress** | Cracking, movement of monoliths, leakage at joints | Crackmeters, joint meters, piezometers, survey |
| **Seepage through/under appurtenances** | Wet areas near conduits, pipes | Seepage, piezometers near structures |

!!! danger "Internal erosion is the leading cause"
    A large share of embankment dam failures involve **internal erosion** — the
    gradual transport of soil particles by seepage. It often begins quietly and
    accelerates, so early detection through seepage *and* pore-pressure monitoring
    is critical.

## 2.3 Embankment dams

For embankment dams, the dominant concerns are:

- **Internal erosion** through the embankment, foundation, or at the interface with
  conduits and walls.
- **Sliding** of the upstream or downstream slope, especially during rapid drawdown
  or rapid fill.
- **Foundation seepage and piping**, particularly through erodible foundations.
- **Liquefaction** of saturated cohesionless foundations or embankments under
  seismic loading.
- **Overtopping** during extreme floods.

The classic warning signs — **sinkholes, springs, muddy or turbid seepage,
unexplained settlement, and slope cracking** — are exactly what visual
surveillance and instrumentation are deployed to detect early.

## 2.4 Concrete and masonry dams

For concrete gravity, arch, and buttress dams, the concerns shift to:

- **Structural cracking** and movement of monoliths.
- **Foundation movement** and uplift pressure beneath the base.
- **Leakage through joints, galleries, and grout curtains**.
- **Aging of appurtenances** (outlets, gates, conduits).

Uplift pressure (pore pressure beneath the dam base) is a first-order design and
monitoring quantity for gravity dams.

## 2.5 The value of a performance baseline

Many failure modes develop slowly. The first years of operation establish the
**baseline** — the range of normal response to seasonal reservoir cycles and
temperatures. Deviations from that baseline are far easier to detect when the
baseline exists ([Chapter 11](chapter-11-data-management.md)).

## 2.6 From failure mode to measurement

For each credible PFM, the engineer defines:

1. The **indicator** (what would change — e.g., seepage rate, piezometric head).
2. The **location** where the indicator is most informative.
3. The **instrument** that measures it.
4. The **threshold** that triggers review ([Chapter 9](chapter-09-automated.md)).

This mapping is the bridge from Chapter 2 to Chapter 4.

## 2.7 Key takeaways

- Plan monitoring from credible failure modes, not from a catalog of instruments.
- Internal erosion and slope instability dominate embankment-dam risk.
- Uplift and structural cracking matter most for concrete dams.
- Establish a baseline early; deviations from it are the signal.
