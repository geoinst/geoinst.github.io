# Chapter 1 — Introduction

## 1.1 Objectives

**Scope.** This manual covers the design, construction, and monitoring of two
families of reinforced-soil structure used on highway projects:

- **MSE walls** — *mechanically stabilized earth* walls, in which soil is
  reinforced and the reinforced mass presents a *vertical or near-vertical* face.
- **RSS** — *reinforced soil slopes*, in which soil is reinforced and the
  reinforced mass presents a *sloping* face (typically steeper than the soil
  could stand unreinforced, but flatter than a wall).

Both are built from the same three ingredients — **frictional fill**,
**tensile reinforcement**, and a **facing** — and both rely on the same
soil–reinforcement interaction. They differ in geometry, in the failure modes
that govern them, and therefore in how they are designed.

**Source documents.** The manual consolidates and updates a line of earlier FHWA
and AASHTO work, principally:

- *Reinforced Soil Structures, Vol. I (Design and Construction Guidelines) and
  Vol. II (Summary of Research and Systems Information)* — FHWA-RD-89-043.
- *Geosynthetic Design and Construction Guidelines* — FHWA-HI-95-038.
- *Design and Construction Monitoring of Mechanically Stabilized Earth
  Structures* — FHWA, 1994.
- *Guidelines for Design, Specification, and Contracting of Geosynthetic
  Mechanically Stabilized Earth Slopes on Firm Foundations* — FHWA-SA-93-025.
- **AASHTO** *Bridge Design Specifications*, Section 5.8 (and its interims),
  which supplies the load and resistance framework the design chapters follow.

This manual is itself the update of **FHWA-SA-96-071** (Demonstration Project
No. 82, *Ground Improvement*).

**Terminology.** The manual fixes a vocabulary that is used consistently
throughout:

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **MSE wall (MSEW)** | A reinforced-soil mass with a vertical or near-vertical facing |
| **Reinforced soil slope (RSS)** | A reinforced-soil mass with a sloping face |
| **Reinforcement** | The tensile elements — steel strips, bar mats, welded wire grids, geotextiles, geogrids |
| **Reinforced fill** | The frictional soil placed *within* the reinforced zone |
| **Retained fill / retained soil** | The soil *behind* the reinforced zone, which the structure holds back |
| **Facing** | The visible element that retains the face of the reinforced fill — concrete panels, modular blocks, wrapped geosynthetic, or vegetated wrap |
| **Foundation soil** | The in-situ soil beneath the reinforced mass |
| **Extensible / inextensible reinforcement** | Whether the reinforcement strains enough to redistribute load (geosynthetics) or is effectively rigid relative to the soil (steel) |
| **Design life** | The period over which the structure must perform, including the durability of the reinforcement |

## 1.2 Historical development

Reinforced soil as an engineered system begins with **Henri Vidal's** *Reinforced
Earth* patent in the 1960s: the observation that a frictional fill reinforced with
closely spaced, frictionally bonded strips behaves as a coherent, anisotropic
mass with *apparent cohesion*. The first major applications were in France and
then the United States, and the concept spread in two directions:

- **Metallic reinforcement** (steel strips and grids) matured into standard
  proprietary MSE wall systems, with published design methods and decades of
  performance records.
- **Geosynthetic reinforcement** (geotextiles and geogrids) followed, first for
  slopes and then for walls and abutments, and opened the technique to a much
  wider range of fills and site conditions.

In parallel, the design methods converged on the framework this manual uses:
**external stability** of the reinforced mass treated as a rigid body, **internal
stability** of each reinforcement layer against tension and pullout, and
**global stability** of the whole slope or wall system. The manual's contribution
is to state those methods uniformly for both metallic and geosynthetic
reinforcement, and to carry them through to specifications and field inspection.

## 1.3 Key takeaways

- MSE walls and reinforced soil slopes share **principles** but not **failure
  modes** — design them differently.
- The reinforcement is **gripped by friction**, not bolted to a rigid structure.
- The manual follows AASHTO's **load-and-resistance** framework and states design
  uniformly for **metallic and geosynthetic** reinforcement.
- The structure's **design life** and the reinforcement's **durability** are
  design inputs, not afterthoughts — see
  [Chapter 3](chapter-03-soil-reinforcement-principles.md).
