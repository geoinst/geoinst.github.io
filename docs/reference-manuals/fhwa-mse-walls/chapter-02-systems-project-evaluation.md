# Chapter 2 — Systems and Project Evaluation

## 2.1 Applications

Reinforced soil is used wherever a soil mass must be made steeper, stronger, or
more stable than it would be unreinforced, but where a conventional concrete
structure would be too costly, too heavy, or too slow to build. The manual groups
applications into three families:

- **MSE walls** — highway embankment widening, grade separations, bridge
  abutments and wing walls, marine and waterfront walls, and urban retaining
  walls where the right-of-way is tight.
- **Reinforced soil slopes** — new cut or fill slopes built steeper than the soil
  would naturally stand; repair of failed or creeping slopes; widening of an
  existing roadway where the toe cannot move outward.
- **Hybrid and specialty uses** — reinforced soil over soft ground, reinforced
  embankments over piles, and structures combining MSE with conventional
  elements.

## 2.2 Advantages and disadvantages

**Advantages of MSE walls**

- **Cost and speed.** Precast facing and granular fill are cheap and fast
  compared with cast-in-place concrete walls.
- **Flexibility.** The facing tolerates differential settlement far better than a
  rigid wall, and can be built to accommodate curves, steps, and complex
  geometry.
- **Constructibility.** The system is built from the bottom up with small
  equipment and no large formwork or curing time.
- **Aesthetics.** Facing textures and finishes are easy to vary.

**Advantages of reinforced soil slopes**

- **Uses site fill.** Marginal on-site soil can often be used in the reinforced
  zone, reducing import and disposal.
- **Vegetation.** The sloping face can be vegetated, so the structure can blend
  into the landscape — a decisive advantage in environmentally sensitive areas.
- **Repairs.** A slope can be stabilized without a large structural wall.

**Disadvantages**

- **Reinforced fill quality matters.** The fill in the reinforced zone must meet
  gradation, plasticity, and electrochemical limits — it is not "any soil".
- **Corrosion and durability.** Steel reinforcement must be protected against
  aggressive backfill and water; geosynthetics must resist creep, installation
  damage, and chemical ageing.
- **Drainage is mandatory.** The design assumes free-draining fill; a blocked or
  omitted drainage system can produce hydrostatic pressures the design never
  contemplated.
- **Deformation.** MSE walls move — the facing tilts and the wall bulges outward
  slightly — and the design must accommodate this, especially where utilities or
  structures sit behind the wall.
- **Not for every site.** Soft or compressible foundations, aggressive
  groundwater, or tight tolerances may rule reinforced soil out.

## 2.3 Relative costs

Reinforced soil is normally the low-cost option within a band of wall heights —
the manual presents cost comparisons showing MSE walls becoming competitive with
conventional retaining walls above roughly a few metres, and reinforced soil
slopes becoming competitive where the required slope angle is modest and site
fill is available. Cost is driven by fill availability, facing type,
reinforcement quantity, and foundation treatment rather than by height alone. The
manual's cost figures (Appendix C, and Chapter 7's project cost estimates) are
dated and must be re-based to local prices.

## 2.4 Description of MSE/RSS systems

**Systems differentiation.** The first question is whether the reinforcement is
**inextensible** or **extensible**:

- **Inextensible** (steel strips, bar mats, welded wire grids) strains very
  little relative to the soil, so the soil's own deformation mobilizes the
  reinforcement's tension. Design uses a *coherent-gravity* model with a
  Rankine-type active earth pressure and a *maximum tensile force line*.
- **Extensible** (geotextiles, geogrids) strains comparably to the soil, so
  internal strain compatibility governs the distribution of load. Design uses a
  *tie-back wedge* model with a coherent-gravity stress distribution that is more
  uniform with depth.

The manual treats both within one framework but flags the different stress
distributions and the different pullout and connection models.

**Types of systems.** MSE wall systems are differentiated by facing (precast
concrete panels, modular concrete blocks, wrapped geosynthetic, timber, or
welded-wire) and by reinforcement (strips, grids, bar mats, geotextiles,
geogrids). Reinforced soil slope systems are differentiated mainly by how the
face is treated (bare wrap, vegetated wrap, armored wrap).

**Facing systems.** Facing types include:

- **Segmental precast concrete panels** — the classic MSE wall face, attached to
  the reinforcement by mechanical connections or friction.
- **Concrete modular block (MBW)** — dry-stacked block units with pins, cores, or
  tails that connect to the reinforcement.
- **Flexible (wrapped) facings** — geosynthetic wrapped around the face fill and
  held by a return wrap; sometimes with a protective mesh or shotcrete skin.
- **Temporary facings** — used during construction, later covered.

**Reinforcement types.** Steel: smooth or ribbed strips, bar mats, welded wire
grids, and (in some systems) steel anchors. Geosynthetic: woven and non-woven
geotextiles, and uniaxial geogrids. Selection depends on strength, stiffness,
durability, connection to the facing, and cost.

**Reinforced backfill materials.** The reinforced fill must be frictional and
free-draining: typically a granular soil meeting limits on fines content,
plasticity index, gradation, and electrochemical aggressivity. Where a suitable
granular fill is unavailable, a lower-quality fill may be acceptable only with
additional design measures (increased reinforcement, extra corrosion protection,
or drainage layers).

**Miscellaneous materials.** Facing joint materials, bearing pads, drainage
aggregate and pipes, filter geotextiles, and erosion-control materials.

## 2.5 Site evaluation

**Site exploration.** A reinforced-soil project needs the same subsurface
information as any retaining structure: stratigraphy, groundwater regime, and
the engineering properties of the foundation soil.

**Field reconnaissance.** Walk the site: identify slope geometry, seepage,
existing instability, drainage paths, utilities, right-of-way, and access for
construction equipment.

**Subsurface exploration.** Borings, test pits, and in-situ tests establish the
foundation profile and the depth to competent bearing strata — decisive for
settlement and global stability.

**Laboratory testing.** Strength and consolidation testing of foundation soils,
and gradation, plasticity, compaction, and electrochemical testing of candidate
reinforced fills.

## 2.6 Project evaluation

The choice among a conventional wall, an MSE wall, and a reinforced slope turns
on several factors:

- **Structure selection factors** — required height and face batter, foundation
  conditions, fill availability, and construction access.
- **Geologic and topographic conditions** — the presence of soft, collapsible, or
  expansive strata; slope stability of the surrounding ground; seismic setting.
- **Environmental conditions** — aggressive groundwater or soil chemistry
  (chlorides, sulphates, acidity), which governs steel corrosion and geosynthetic
  durability; and ecological constraints, which may favour a vegetated RSS.
- **Size and nature of the structure** — the load the structure must carry
  (including bridge abutment loads) and the required service life.
- **Aesthetics** — the visible face and its context.
- **Questionable applications** — the manual flags situations where reinforced
  soil is a poor fit, including very soft foundations, aggressive environments
  without adequate protection, and cases where tolerances are unrealistically
  tight.

## 2.7 Establishment of project criteria

**Alternates.** Compare at least one conventional solution against the
reinforced-soil option on cost, constructibility, and risk.

**Facing considerations.** Facing type, texture, and jointing are chosen for
performance, durability, and appearance.

**Performance criteria.** Set the limits on facing deformation, differential
settlement, and joint opening that the structure must satisfy — the criteria the
design must meet and the monitoring must verify.

**Design life.** Fix the required service life (commonly 75–100 years for
highway structures), because it directly sets the corrosion allowance for steel
reinforcement and the reduction factors applied to geosynthetic strength.

## 2.8 Construction sequence

- **MSE walls with precast facings** — place the levelling pad, erect the first
  panel course, connect and place reinforcement, place and compact fill in lifts,
  then repeat course by course.
- **MSE walls with flexible facings** — build in lifts, wrapping the geosynthetic
  face around the fill and holding it with a return wrap, then compact.
- **RSS construction** — place reinforcement at the design elevations as the fill
  is raised, benching the foundation, then treat the face (vegetation or armor).

## 2.9 Proprietary aspects

Many MSE systems are proprietary, protected by patents, and supplied with their
own design methods, facing details, and connection capacities. The manual
therefore distinguishes **materials** (which must meet specification regardless
of supplier) from **special applications** (where the system supplier's own
design and test data are needed). This is the origin of the *end-result* and
*supplier-design* contracting approaches in
[Chapter 8](chapter-08-contracting-specifications.md).

## 2.10 Key takeaways

- Choose the structure family first: **MSE wall** (vertical face) or **RSS**
  (sloping face) — they fail differently and are designed differently.
- Classify the reinforcement as **extensible or inextensible**; it changes the
  internal-stability model.
- **Fill quality, corrosion/durability, and drainage** are the three recurring
  failure causes — evaluate them at the project stage.
- **Design life** is a design input that propagates into every durability factor.
