# Chapter 6 — Reinforced (Steepened) Soil Slopes: Project Evaluation

## 6.1 Introduction

A **reinforced soil slope (RSS)** is a soil slope, steeper than the soil could
stand unreinforced, whose stability is provided by horizontal layers of
reinforcement extending back from the face into the stable ground behind the
critical slip surface. Unlike an MSE wall, the face is *sloped*, and the
structure's stability is a **global** slope-stability problem, not a
rigid-body problem.

RSS is the natural solution when the required slope angle is moderate, when site
fill is available, when vegetation is wanted, and when the foundation is too
weak for a wall. It is also the standard repair technique for a failed or
creeping slope.

## 6.2 Reinforced soil slope systems

**Types of systems.** RSS systems are differentiated by:

- **Reinforcement type** — geosynthetic (geotextile or geogrid), which is the
  most common, or metallic (grids, bar mats) for specific applications.
- **Face treatment** — bare wrap, **vegetated** wrap (the dominant form, often
  called a *vegetated reinforced soil slope*, VRSS), or **armored** wrap.
- **Construction staging** — built up as a new fill, or used to repair an
  existing slope by excavating in benches.

**Construction materials.** The reinforced fill must again be frictional and
free-draining; the reinforcement must meet the same creep, installation-damage,
and durability requirements as for walls; the face treatment materials (erosion
control mats, vegetation, or armor) are chosen for the environment and the
design life.

## 6.3 Design approach

**Use considerations.** RSS is preferred where the required face angle is
relatively flat (so the reinforcement is efficient), where the foundation is
weak, where vegetation is required, and where the failure mode is a shallow or
intermediate slide rather than a deep-seated one.

**Design of reinforcement for compaction aid.** A lightly reinforced slope may be
used simply to allow compaction of a steep face fill — the reinforcement's role
is to hold the face during construction, not to provide long-term stability.

**Design of reinforcement for steepening slopes and slope repair.** The
reinforcement is designed to raise the factor of safety of the critical slip
surface to the required value. The mechanics are the **modified limit-equilibrium
analysis with reinforcement** described in
[Chapter 7](chapter-07-design-rss.md).

**Computer-assisted design.** Because the critical surface is unknown, RSS design
is normally done with a slope-stability program that searches for the critical
surface and accounts for reinforcement forces.

**Evaluation of external stability.** The reinforced slope can still fail
*externally* — by sliding along a weak foundation layer, by bearing failure, or
by a deep-seated slide passing below the reinforced zone — so external stability
must be checked independently of the internal reinforcement design.

## 6.4 Construction sequence

Build the reinforced slope in lifts: prepare and bench the foundation, place the
reinforcement at the design elevations, place and compact fill, wrap the face,
then repeat. The face is protected during construction and vegetated or armored
afterward.

## 6.5 Treatment of the outward face

**Grass-type vegetation.** Seed or hydromulch the wrapped face; simple and
cheap, but limited to gentle face angles and mild climates, and vulnerable before
the vegetation is established.

**Soil bioengineering (woody vegetation).** Live cuttings, brush layers, and
other bioengineering techniques are used on steeper faces and in wetter
climates; the roots reinforce the face soil while the plants provide surface
protection.

**Armored.** Where vegetation will not survive, or where the face is exposed to
flow, the face is armored — with rock, articulated concrete blocks, or a
protective mesh or shotcrete skin over the wrap.

## 6.6 Design details

- **Guardrail and traffic barriers** — carry the impact load to a structural
  element, not into the slope face.
- **Drainage** — intercept surface water above the slope and convey it away;
  control subsurface water so it does not raise pore pressures in the reinforced
  zone.
- **Obstructions** — utilities and drainage structures interrupt reinforcement;
  detail around them.

## 6.7 Case histories

The manual documents five built projects that illustrate the range of RSS
applications:

- **The Dickey Lake Roadway Grade Improvement Project** — a reinforced slope used
  to widen and steepen a roadway grade.
- **Salmon–Lost Trail Roadway Widening Project** — roadway widening with a
  reinforced slope where the toe could not move.
- **Cannon Creek Alternate Embankment Construction Project** — an alternative
  embankment built with reinforcement.
- **Pennsylvania SR 54 Roadway Repair Project** — repair of a failing roadway
  slope.
- **Massachusetts Turnpike — Use of Soil Bioengineering** — a bioengineered face
  treatment on a reinforced slope.

## 6.8 Standard RSS designs

As for walls, the manual provides **standard RSS designs** for common geometries
and conditions, so routine slopes need not be redesigned from scratch.

## 6.9 Key takeaways

- An RSS is a **slope-stability** problem, not a rigid-body problem — the critical
  slip surface governs.
- The same reinforcement as a wall is used, but the **face is sloped and usually
  vegetated**.
- Check **internal** (reinforcement) and **external** (foundation) stability
  separately.
- The **face treatment** (vegetation or armor) is a design decision driven by
  climate, angle, and exposure.
- **Drainage** is the most common cause of RSS distress.
