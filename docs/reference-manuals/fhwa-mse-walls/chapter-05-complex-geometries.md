# Chapter 5 — MSE Walls with Complex Geometries

## 5.1 Bridge abutments

An **MSE abutment** carries the bridge bearing loads in addition to the earth
pressure, and those loads enter the reinforced mass at the top — where the
reinforcement is least able to redistribute them. This is the most demanding MSE
application, and the manual treats it in detail.

**MSEW abutments on spread footings.** The footing bears on the reinforced fill
(or on the foundation behind it), and its loads (vertical, horizontal, and
moment) are carried into the reinforced mass and the foundation. The design must
check:

- **Bearing** under the footing, including eccentricity from the bridge loads.
- **Internal stability** with the footing load added as a large surcharge — the
  tension in the upper reinforcement layers rises sharply.
- **Sliding** of the reinforced mass under the horizontal bridge load.
- **Global stability** through the abutment and its foundation.
- **Settlement** of the footing and the approach, and the **tolerance** for
  differential movement between the bridge seat and the approach slab.

**MSEW abutments on pile foundations.** Where the foundation is too weak for a
spread footing, the abutment is carried on **piles**, and the MSE wall is built
around them. This decouples bridge settlement from wall settlement but creates
its own problems: the piles interrupt the reinforcement, the pile cap generates
concentrated loads, and the design must ensure the reinforcement is not damaged
by pile driving or by the pile cap's movement.

## 5.2 Superimposed (terraced) walls

Where two or more MSE walls are stacked with a berm between them, the lower wall
carries the surcharge of the upper one. The manual gives **design rules** for
superimposed walls: the offset between walls, the reinforcement lengths in each,
and the way the upper wall's load is spread onto the lower. Ignoring the
interaction — designing each wall as if it stood alone — is a classic error.

## 5.3 Walls with uneven reinforcement lengths

Where the reinforcement length must vary along the wall (because of obstructions,
geometry, or variable loads), the design must still provide a **continuous**
reinforced mass and check the transition zones where the length changes, because
the shorter sections attract less pullout resistance and can act as weak points.

## 5.4 Back-to-back walls

Where two walls face each other with a narrow soil mass between them, the
reinforced zones may **overlap**. The manual distinguishes cases by the ratio of
the separation to the wall height:

- **Wide separation** — each wall is designed independently.
- **Narrow separation** — the reinforced zones overlap and the walls act as a
  single composite; the design must treat the mass as one.
- **Very narrow separation** — the two facings effectively form a single wall
  with reinforcement crossing the full width.

## 5.5 Details

Abutment-specific details include the **bridge seat**, the **approach slab**
connection, the **bearing pad** arrangement, and the **drainage** behind and
through the abutment — all of which must tolerate the wall's outward deformation
without distress.

## 5.6 Design example, bridge abutment

The manual works a full abutment design — geometry, bridge loads, external
stability, internal stability with the footing surcharge, and the resulting
reinforcement layout — by hand and by computer program.

## 5.7 Key takeaways

- An **abutment is a wall plus a concentrated top load** — the load path into the
  reinforcement and the foundation must be explicit.
- **Piled abutments** decouple settlement but interrupt reinforcement; detail the
  interface carefully.
- **Superimposed and back-to-back walls interact** — design the system, not the
  individual walls.
- **Uneven reinforcement lengths** create transition zones that must be checked.
- Tolerate **deformation and differential settlement** at the bridge seat, or the
  approach will crack.
