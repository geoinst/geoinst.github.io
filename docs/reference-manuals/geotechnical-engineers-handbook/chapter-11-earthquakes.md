# Chapter 11 — Earthquakes and Construction Works

Chapter XI introduces earthquakes for the civil engineer: what they are, how they are
described, how they are mapped in Vietnam, and how they enter the design of bridges and
buildings — with the soil-mechanics problems, above all **liquefaction**, treated
explicitly.

## 11.1 An overview of earthquakes

### 11.1.1 Definition and concepts

An earthquake is the result of deformation in the Earth's crust. Strain energy
accumulates until, at some point, the resistance is weakest and the stress exceeds
equilibrium: rupture then propagates rapidly, radiating seismic waves.

Key terms:

- **Focus** (chấn tiêu) — the point within the Earth where rupture begins.
- **Epicenter** (chấn tâm) — the point on the surface vertically above the focus.
- **Fault** (đứt gãy), **active fault** (đứt gãy hoạt động) — the fracture along which
  displacement occurs.

### 11.2.4 Characteristics of seismic vibration

Two body waves carry the energy away from the focus: the **P wave** (compression —
"push–pull", the first to arrive) and the **S wave** (shear, slower). It is the S wave
that does most structural damage, and — critically for the geotechnical engineer — **S
waves do not travel through liquid**. Surface waves follow and are the slowest.

![Figure: geology-seismic-hammer](../../assets/figures/geology/seismic-hammer.png)

**Figure.** The principle behind every seismic method in geotechnical investigation: a
hammer blow (or a borehole source) sends elastic waves into the ground, and the arrival
times of the P and S waves at a receiver give the wave velocities of the material.
*© Steven Earle, CC BY 4.0 — Physical Geology, 2nd ed., Fig. 9.1.1.*

![Figure: geology-wave-velocities](../../assets/figures/geology/wave-velocities.png)

**Figure.** Typical P-wave (red) and S-wave (blue) velocities in sediments and in solid
crustal rock. Velocities in **soft sediments** are an order of magnitude lower than in
rock — which is why the same earthquake shakes a soft-soil site far more violently than a
rock site.
*© Steven Earle, CC BY 4.0, after the US Environmental Protection Agency (public domain)
— Physical Geology, 2nd ed., Fig. 9.1.3.*

![Figure: geology-wave-shadow-zones](../../assets/figures/geology/wave-shadow-zones.jpg)

**Figure.** Propagation of seismic waves through the mantle and core. S waves are stopped
at the core–mantle boundary, leaving an S-wave shadow on the far side of the Earth — the
same physics that lets geophysics map a **soft or liquid layer** beneath a site.
*© Steven Earle, CC BY 4.0 — Physical Geology, 2nd ed., Fig. 9.1.6.*

### 11.2.6 Consequences of earthquakes

Beyond direct shaking damage, an earthquake **destabilises slopes** and can trigger
widespread landsliding — particularly where the ground has already been saturated by
rain. The 2018 Sapporo failures are the textbook sequence: a typhoon saturated the
volcanic soils, then an M6.6 earthquake triggered thousands of debris flows.

![Figure: geology-hokkaido-landslides](../../assets/figures/geology/hokkaido-landslides.jpg)

**Figure.** Slope failures in the Sapporo area, Japan, after a typhoon (4 Sept 2018) and an
earthquake (6 Sept 2018). Before (July 2017, left) and after (September 2018, right)
Landsat 8 images. Saturation followed by shaking is the classic compound trigger.
*"Landslides in Hokkaido" by Lauren Dauphin, NASA Earth Observatory (public domain) —
Physical Geology, 2nd ed., Fig. 15.1.7.*

### 11.4.3 Soil liquefaction (hiện tượng hóa lỏng)

Under cyclic seismic loading, loose saturated sands can lose their strength and behave
as a liquid. **Liquefaction** is a first-order geotechnical hazard: it destroys bearing
capacity, causes lateral spreading and settlement, and has toppled structures that were
otherwise well built. The handbook presents the conditions, the assessment and the
mitigation.

![Figure: geology-sand-dry-moist-saturated](../../assets/figures/geology/sand-dry-moist-saturated.png)

**Figure.** Why saturation is the precondition for liquefaction: saturated sand is the
**weakest** of the three states, because water under pressure pushes the grains apart.
Cyclic shaking removes the grain-to-grain contact altogether, and the soil behaves as a
liquid.
*© Steven Earle, CC BY 4.0 — Physical Geology, 2nd ed., Fig. 15.1.4.*

## 11.6 Key takeaways

- Strain accumulates in the crust and is released as **rupture and seismic waves**.
- **Magnitude** is a single number for the event; **intensity** varies with location.
- Vietnam **does** have seismicity — the northwest and central highlands in particular.
- **Liquefaction** of loose saturated sand is the signature geotechnical hazard and must
  be assessed wherever it can occur.
- Seismic design needs both the **ground motion** and the **soil response**.
