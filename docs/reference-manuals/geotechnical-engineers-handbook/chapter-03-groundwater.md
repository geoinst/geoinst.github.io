# Chapter 3 — Groundwater

Groundwater cannot be separated from geotechnical investigation. Chapter III gives the
minimum groundwater theory a geotechnical engineer needs, then describes the main
groundwater types.

## 3.1 Basic concepts

### 3.1.1 The porous medium

To talk about groundwater you must first talk about the **porous medium** (môi trường
rỗng) — the soil or rock in which water moves. Every soil and rock contains voids, and
they arise in two different ways:

- **Interstitial voids** (khe rỗng giữa hạt) — the spaces between individual grains,
  created when the material was deposited.
- **Structural voids** (khoáng rỗng kiến tạo) — voids created later by geological
  processes: fissures, cracks, fractures, root holes, burrows, dissolution.

This distinction matters because the two void systems behave differently and must be
described differently.

### 3.1.2 Tortuosity

**Tortuosity** (độ uốn lượn) describes how crooked the actual flow path is compared
with the straight-line distance — it reduces the effective flow through a medium.

### 3.1.3 Void ratio and related characteristics

The **degree of voiding** (mức độ rỗng) and the associated parameters (void ratio $e$,
porosity $n$) quantify how much of the medium is void.

### 3.1.4 Degree of permeability

**Permeability** (mức độ thấm) is the capacity of the medium to transmit water. It is
the single most important property for seepage, drainage and consolidation problems.

![Figure: geology-hydraulic-conductivity](../../assets/figures/geology/hydraulic-conductivity.png)

**Figure.** Hydraulic conductivity $K$ (m/s) for unconsolidated materials (red) and rocks
(blue) — a range of some twelve orders of magnitude. **Clay is porous but nearly
impermeable**, because almost all of its water is held as bound water on the grain
surfaces and cannot flow.
*© Steven Earle, CC BY 4.0 — Physical Geology, 2nd ed., Fig. 14.1.2.*

### 3.1.5 Moisture content and degree of saturation

**Moisture content** (độ ẩm) and **degree of saturation** (độ bão hoà) describe how
much of the void space is filled with water.

![Figure: geology-sand-dry-moist-saturated](../../assets/figures/geology/sand-dry-moist-saturated.png)

**Figure.** Dry, moist and saturated sand. Surface tension between grains makes **moist**
sand the strongest of the three; **saturated** sand is the weakest, because water under
pressure pushes the grains apart and reduces friction. This is the mechanism behind
liquefaction (Chapter 11).
*© Steven Earle, CC BY 4.0 — Physical Geology, 2nd ed., Fig. 15.1.4.*

### 3.1.6 Surface tension

**Surface tension** (sức căng bề mặt) is the force that lets water support itself in
narrow voids and is the physical cause of capillarity.

### 3.1.7 Capillary action

**Capillary rise** (hiện tượng mao dẫn) lifts water above the free water table in fine
soils. The capillary zone is *not* saturated in the usual sense, but its negative pore
pressure changes effective stress and therefore strength — a recurring theme in slope
and retaining-wall problems.

## 3.2 The main groundwater types

### 3.2.1 Groundwater in granular sediments

Water held in the pore system of sands, gravels and silts. It is the most predictable
aquifer type and the one in which Darcy's law (Chapter 10) applies most cleanly.

### 3.2.2 Karst water

Water in solution channels and caves within limestone. Karst aquifers are **highly
non-uniform**: flow is concentrated in conduits, and the same formation can be
impermeable a metre away from a major channel. Karst water is a first-order hazard for
foundations, tunnels and dam abutments in Vietnam's limestone regions.

### 3.2.3 Distribution where permeability is uniform

Where the medium is uniformly permeable, groundwater distribution can be described
analytically — the basis for the flow-net and seepage calculations in Chapter 10.

### 3.2.4 Aquifers, aquitards and confining layers

A body of ground that transmits water usefully is an **aquifer** (tầng chứa nước); one
that does not is an **aquitard** or **confining layer** (tầng cách nước). An aquifer
open to the surface is **unconfined** (không áp); one capped by a lower-permeability
layer is **confined** (có áp) — and the pore pressure in a confined aquifer is what
makes piezometers read above the level of the aquifer itself.

![Figure: geology-aquifers-confining](../../assets/figures/geology/aquifers-confining.png)

**Figure.** A cross-section of aquifers and confining layers, with relative permeabilities
given as hydraulic conductivity $K$ (m/s). The grey layer ($K = 10^{-4}$) confines the

the regional aquitard.

## 3.4 Key takeaways

- **Interstitial** and **structural** voids are different systems and must be described
  separately.
- **Permeability** is the property that governs seepage, drainage and consolidation.
- The **capillary zone** changes effective stress even though it is not saturated.
- **Karst water** is highly non-uniform and is a major hazard in limestone terrain.
