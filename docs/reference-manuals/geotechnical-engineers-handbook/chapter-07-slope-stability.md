# Chapter 7 — Slope Stability Analysis

Chapter VII concerns the stability of slopes — both **natural** slopes and **man-made**
slopes. Slope failure can have very serious consequences for property, works and
people, so the chapter first classifies the ways a slope can move, then presents the
methods for judging whether a slope is stable.

The handbook is explicit about a limitation: **precisely determining the factor of
safety of a slope is difficult.** It requires a careful and thorough study of the soil
conditions, the mechanics, and the topography, in order to identify the governing causes
and conditions, select representative parameters, and choose a defensible factor of
safety.

## 7.1 Classifying slope movements

Two things decide whether a slope stands: the **angle of the slope** and the **strength
of the material on it**. Both are then modified by water and by the orientation of any
planes of weakness — bedding, foliation or joints — relative to the slope face. Where
those planes are **parallel** to the slope, the slope is at its most unstable; where they
are **perpendicular**, it is at its most stable.

## 7.2 The main movement types

### 7.2.1 Rockfall — rolling and falling of rock blocks

Rockfall is driven by **frost wedging and unloading** in steep, jointed rock. The
weathered debris accumulates as a **talus** cone at the toe — a loose, poorly sorted and
often metastable deposit, and itself a hazard if it is later cut or loaded.

### 7.2.8 Creep and debris flow

**Creep** (trượt từ từ) is the slow, imperceptible downslope movement of soil and rock
debris. It does not fail suddenly, but it is cumulative, and it is **readable in the
vegetation**: trunks that are tilted and then turn upward again — *pistol-butt* trees —
record years of creep on the slope.

A **debris flow** (dòng bùn đá) is the opposite — fast, water-charged and destructive. It
travels in a steep channel as a slurry of water, mud, rock and vegetation, and it is the
mechanism that turns a modest slope failure into a hazard to anything downslope.

## 7.3 Slope stability analysis

Every stability analysis comes down to the same comparison. Gravity pulls a block
vertically down; that force resolves into a **shear force** pushing the block *down the
slope* and a **normal force** pushing it *into the slope*. The slope holds if the
available **shear strength** along the failure surface exceeds the shear force.

![Figure: geology-shear-normal-forces](../../assets/figures/geology/shear-normal-forces.jpg)

**Figure.** Shear and normal components of gravity on slopes of increasing steepness. The
gravitational force is the same in all three cases; only its resolution changes. (a)
shear force $\ll$ shear strength — stable; (b) the two are about equal — marginal; (c)
shear force $\gg$ shear strength — failure.
*© Steven Earle, CC BY 4.0 — Physical Geology, 2nd ed., Fig. 15.1.1.*

## 7.5 Key takeaways

- Slopes are **natural** or **man-made**; the failure mechanisms differ.
- Movements fall into **rockfall, sliding, heave/squeeze, and water erosion**.
- Sliding is classified by the shape of the **failure surface** — plane or circular arc.
- **Exactly** determining the factor of safety is hard; the work is in the soil
  characterisation and parameter choice, not just the arithmetic.
- The **circular-arc** method is the workhorse and leads into the Bishop method used for
  staged embankment construction in Chapter 9.
